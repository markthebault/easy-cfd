Day 1 / 01 / Foundations

Forces, axes, and the design objective

You will distinguish drag, lift, downforce, and moments, and translate a design goal into measurable quantities.

Air loads every exposed surface

Air exerts pressure normal to a surface and viscous shear along it. Add those small forces over the complete exposed surface and you obtain the total aerodynamic force. Air also produces a moment because those forces act at different positions. A wing is therefore not the only source of downforce: the body, floor, wheels, and cooling flow all contribute. Conversely, adding a part changes the pressure on existing surfaces, so the total car change can differ substantially from the force on the new part.

In a car-fixed view the air approaches the nose and moves toward the rear. EasyCFD uses a nose pointing toward -X, airflow toward +X at zero yaw, and +Z upward. Positive lift is upward force. Define positive downforce as Naero = -L. If the body and wheels make +400 N lift and a wing contributes -150 N in the same installed flow, the whole-car total is +250 N lift. That remaining lift still unloads the tyres. A negative downforce number is uplift under this convention.

F = ∫surface [-p n + τ · n] dS
MO = ∫surface (r - rO) × dF

Here n is the outward normal from the solid into the air, p is pressure, τ is the viscous stress tensor, and O is the chosen moment origin. Uniform ambient pressure cancels on a closed surface. That cancellation is one reason holes, missing faces, and incorrectly counted internal surfaces can corrupt an integration. Gauge pressure is convenient, but the same reference must apply everywhere you integrate. Pressure on the underside also pushes into the solid; whether it contributes upward or downward depends on both pressure relative to ambient and the surface normal.

Which improvement do you actually want?

A road car may benefit from reducing drag while keeping axle lift within acceptable bounds. A track car may accept more drag for useful downforce, provided the balance, ride-height sensitivity, and structural loads are acceptable. There is no single “more aerodynamic” score. Minimising drag with no constraint can lead to a body that lifts. Maximising downforce with no constraint can produce an unusable straight-line power demand. The engineer's task is to find a feasible package for the operating conditions.

Keep three metrics separate: total drag D, total positive downforce Naero, and the front share of that downforce. Also track side force and yawing moment in crosswind. The ratio Naero/D is an efficiency measure, but a very efficient device that adds almost no load may not meet your target. The incremental ratio ΔNaero/ΔD describes a modification only when ΔD is positive and meaningfully different from zero. Near zero added drag, report the two changes separately; an enormous ratio can hide numerical noise.

Pressure is not weight

A force of 981 N is approximately 100 kgf, meaning the force equivalent of the weight of 100 kg under standard gravity. It does not add 100 kg of vehicle mass or inertia. Aero load disappears at low speed, changes in wind, and may move between axles. Use newtons for mechanics and label any kilogram display as an equivalent load at a specified speed. You cannot use that number alone to specify suspension, tyre grip, or structural strength.

Worked example

A baseline has D = 420 N and L = +180 N. A package has D = 500 N and L = -120 N at the same speed. Added downforce is -(-120) - (-180) = 300 N, added drag is 80 N, and incremental efficiency is 3.75. The package's total downforce is only 120 N. Mixing those two downforce quantities exaggerates the final load.

Check your understanding: a wing makes 200 N downforce, but the car has 100 N lift. Is the car pushed onto the road?

No. The wing's negative lift is included in the total. Total L = +100 N still unloads the tyres. You need the whole-car force and moment, not just the wing result.

Day 1 / 02 / Foundations

Pressure, momentum, and Bernoulli

You will explain a pressure field without relying on the equal-transit-time myth or treating Bernoulli as a universal solver.

Follow a parcel, then follow the forces

Air changes velocity because forces act on it. A pressure gradient is one of those forces; viscosity is another. A curved streamline requires acceleration toward its centre of curvature and therefore a corresponding force. Around a car, geometry and the boundary conditions establish a connected velocity and pressure field. You cannot decide that the air must move twice as fast under a car just because a local gap halves. The amount of air entering the gap can also change, and flow can escape sideways.

Conservation of mass in a steady streamtube gives ρUA = constant when velocity is represented by an appropriate area average. For constant density, a smaller cross-section requires higher mean speed if the same mass flow stays in that streamtube. A car's floor is an open three-dimensional system, unlike a sealed pipe. The streamtube boundaries themselves depend on pressure, leakage, wheels, and yaw. The continuity equation is a constraint on a solution, not permission to prescribe a solution from a sketch.

p + ½ρU² + ρgz = constant along a streamline
Cp = (p - p∞) / q∞   ;   q∞ = ½ρU∞²

The first expression is the steady, inviscid, incompressible Bernoulli relation without work or heat input. At car scales the elevation term in air is usually small compared with dynamic pressure. Outside strongly viscous regions, along a streamline from a common freestream, it provides a useful way to relate local pressure and speed. It cannot restore energy lost to wall friction and mixing, predict where a diffuser will separate, or determine mass flow through a radiator. Streamlines coming from different sources need not have the same total pressure.

Stagnation and suction

At a well-behaved incompressible stagnation point, local speed approaches zero and static pressure approaches freestream total pressure; Cp is approximately +1. A value of Cp = -0.6 denotes pressure 0.6 dynamic-pressure units below freestream static pressure. It is not a negative absolute pressure. With q = 500 Pa the gauge pressure is -300 Pa, still very close to the roughly 100,000 Pa ambient absolute pressure. Suction on the underside can pull a floor downward; suction on a roof commonly pulls the roof upward. The colour alone does not determine the force direction.

A turning flow and a pressure integral are consistent descriptions of the same interaction. A downward-loaded wing pushes air upward on average; the reaction on the wing is downward. The detailed pressure distribution is how that reaction is transmitted to the solid. Calling one explanation “Newton” and the other “Bernoulli” does not make them competing mechanisms. A correct flow satisfies momentum conservation as well as its applicable energy relation.

What a wing does not require

Air parcels that separate at a leading edge are not obliged to meet again at the trailing edge at the same time. An inclined flat plate can generate lift without two different path lengths. A symmetric airfoil can generate lift at incidence. In a useful attached-flow model, circulation, trailing-edge behaviour, camber, and angle of attack jointly describe the velocity field. Viscosity matters even when most of the integrated lift comes from pressure because it governs boundary layers, separation, and the selected flow state.

Worked example

At U∞ = 30 m/s and ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, q∞ = 551.25 Pa. Ideal acceleration to 36 m/s gives Cp = 1 - (36/30)² = -0.44 and a static-pressure change of -242.55 Pa. This calculation assumes unchanged total pressure along that streamline. In a lossy floor passage, the same Cp cannot be used to infer speed without accounting for the loss.

Check: can a larger blue low-pressure patch on the roof mean more downforce?

Not by itself. Lower pressure on an upward-facing roof often increases upward force. Integrate pressure with the surface normals over the complete geometry and include shear.

Day 1 / 03 / Foundations

Speed, coefficients, and similarity

You will convert coefficients into forces and power, and decide which dimensionless groups matter.

Dynamic pressure is the natural force scale

Define CD = D/(q∞Aref) and CL = L/(q∞Aref). A coefficient removes the explicit density, speed-squared, and area scale, making comparison easier. It does not remove every dependence on speed: Reynolds number, ground clearance, yaw, and flow state can still change the coefficient. A declared reference area is part of the result. Two Cd values using different areas cannot be compared directly. The product CdA, often called drag area, is particularly useful for comparing cars of different size.

The usual whole-car reporting area is frontal area; a wing commonly uses planform area. Converting is simple: Cd,car = Cd,wing × Swing/Acar for the same dimensional wing drag and freestream q. If the local wing sees a different dynamic pressure, use its actual force first and then divide by the car's reference q and area. Never insert a car-area lift coefficient directly into a wing's induced-drag formula.

D = ½ρU² CDA   ;   L = ½ρU² CLA
Paero = D U in still air

Reynolds number: inertia versus viscosity

Re = UL/ν, where ν is kinematic viscosity and L is a declared length. Use car length for a global comparison and wing chord for section behaviour. At 100 km/h, L = 4.2 m and ν = 1.5 × 10^-5 m²/s give Re ≈ 7.78 million. The same air over a 0.32 m wing chord has Re ≈ 593,000. The wing's surface roughness, transition, and laminar separation may therefore matter differently from those of the body. A reduced-scale wind-tunnel model does not automatically reproduce the full-size car.

A quarter-scale model tested at the same speed has one quarter of the length-based Reynolds number. Matching it in the same air would require four times the speed, which may introduce significant compressibility, change facility limits, and increase loads. Transition trips can deliberately set boundary-layer state in a model test, but they are not a universal correction for mismatched Reynolds number. State what was matched and measure the residual sensitivity.

Mach number and other ratios

Mach number M = U/a compares speed with the speed of sound. With a ≈ 343 m/s at about 20°C, 100 km/h is M ≈ 0.081 and 300 km/h is M ≈ 0.243. Incompressible modeling is often a reasonable starting point at ordinary car speeds, but evaluate local acceleration and the required accuracy rather than treating 0.3 as a hard physics switch. The sound speed and density both vary with atmospheric conditions.

Dimensionless geometry is equally important: wing height/chord, floor clearance/length, diffuser exit/throat area, wheel diameter/length, and splitter overhang/clearance. Strouhal number St = fL/U is useful when comparing wake frequencies. A solver's pseudo-time iteration history is not automatically a physical frequency record. You need a time-accurate method and adequate sampling before inferring an unsteady loading frequency.

Worked example

At 100 km/h with A = 2.2 m², Cd = 0.32, Cl = -0.40, and ρ = 1.225, q = 472.61 Pa, D ≈ 332.7 N, downforce ≈ 415.9 N, and still-air aero power ≈ 9.24 kW. At 200 km/h the forces are four times larger and the power is eight times larger if the coefficients and geometry stay constant.

Check: car A has Cd = 0.30 with A = 2.5 m²; car B has Cd = 0.33 with A = 2.1 m². Which has less drag at the same speed?

Car B: its drag area is 0.693 m² versus 0.750 m² for car A. Cd alone would give the wrong ranking.

Day 1 / 04 / Foundations

Boundary layers and separation

You will recognise pressure recovery, understand why flow can detach, and distinguish turbulence from separation.

The slow air near a wall changes everything

For an ordinary stationary solid wall, the fluid velocity at the wall equals the wall velocity. In the car-fixed frame the body is stationary, while the road and tyre surface move. Away from the wall the flow approaches an external velocity. Between them lies a boundary layer with strong velocity gradients and viscous shear. It grows along the surface and changes the effective displacement of the outside flow. A boundary layer is not a uniform film of fixed thickness.

A laminar boundary layer has ordered mean motion with relatively weak mixing across its thickness. A turbulent one has velocity fluctuations and stronger momentum transport. A turbulent profile is often fuller and can tolerate stronger pressure recovery before separating, but it generally has more skin friction. Transition does not equal separation; turbulence can exist in an attached layer. Likewise, low turbulence in a plot does not establish that a flow is attached or efficient.

Pressure recovery is an uphill climb

When pressure decreases downstream, the pressure gradient accelerates the external flow. This is a favourable gradient. When pressure increases downstream, it decelerates the flow and is adverse to the near-wall layer. The slowest fluid may lack enough momentum to negotiate that pressure rise. Wall shear approaches zero; reverse flow can develop, and the layer detaches. The exact separation behaviour depends on upstream history, geometry, three-dimensionality, and unsteadiness.

A diffuser intentionally recovers pressure by expanding a passage. A rear body taper also asks air to recover pressure after the roof or sides. Both can succeed when the recovery is gradual enough for the incoming layer, and fail when it is too demanding. Simply making a ramp steeper creates more expansion, but can also create a detached region that blocks effective flow. A longer smooth recovery may cost packaging space or wetted-area friction. This is the origin of a design tradeoff, not a universal “best angle.”

Learn the diagnostic combination

Look for local skin-friction direction, reversed surface flow, near-wall velocity profiles, recirculation, a pressure plateau, and the growth of a wake. A single centreline streamline can miss separation near a diffuser side edge. A mean streamline can conceal intermittent detachment. On a three-dimensional surface, a separation line is not fully described by the simple two-dimensional rule “the tangential velocity gradient is zero.” In practice, surface-flow topology and off-wall sections belong together.

If the app has only a coarse wall model, a colourful surface-friction map may not resolve the actual near-wall physics. Ask whether the grid, wall distance, and turbulence treatment support the feature you are interpreting. A 5 mm Gurney flap or 10 mm tyre-floor gap cannot be judged from a 60 mm field sample just because the geometry viewer draws it sharply.

Useful flat-plate estimates, with narrow scope

For an ideal zero-pressure-gradient flat plate, local laminar estimates include δ/x ≈ 5/√Reₓ and Cfₓ ≈ 0.664/√Reₓ. A common smooth turbulent local estimate is Cfₓ ≈ 0.0592 Reₓ^-1/5 under its fully turbulent assumptions. These are not whole-car drag formulas, not reliable separation criteria, and not a transition model. Use them to get an order of magnitude, then ask why a roof, rotating tyre, or diffuser violates the assumptions.

Worked example

At U = 30 m/s and x = 1 m, Reₓ = 2 million. The ideal laminar thickness estimate is 3.5 mm. A rough turbulent thickness estimate δ/x ≈ 0.37 Reₓ^-1/5 gives about 20 mm. Neither value accounts for the car's pressure gradient or upstream disturbances; their difference explains why boundary-layer state matters for a small gap.

Check: should you make every surface as laminar as possible to reduce drag?

No. Laminar flow can reduce friction but may separate under pressure recovery that an attached turbulent layer would tolerate. You must evaluate the combined friction and pressure-drag change.

Day 1 / 05 / Foundations

Where drag comes from

You will identify plausible drag sources and understand why smoothing a surface may not reduce whole-car drag.

Pressure drag and friction drag

Pressure produces drag when its normal force has a component opposing the car's motion. A blunt front creates relatively high pressure, while the rear wake can leave low base pressure. The incomplete rear recovery creates a large pressure-drag contribution. Skin friction is the streamwise component of tangential stress over the wetted surface. Their relative importance depends on the geometry and conditions. Do not import a fixed percentage from another vehicle to decide what matters on yours.

Roof, sides, underbody, wheels, and cooling passages all influence the wake. A smoother floor may reduce frictional disturbance and improve pressure recovery, but it may also increase flow through a constricted passage, alter lift, or change wheel interactions. A carefully placed spoiler can fix a separation location and sometimes improve base pressure; another one can lower base pressure and increase drag. A clean-looking smoke pattern is a hypothesis about the mechanism, not a measurement of the total drag.

A wake is more than a trail of vortices

The flow behind a car can contain recirculation, shear layers, streamwise vortices, and asymmetric states. Vorticity is local fluid rotation; a coherent vortex is a particular organised structure, not every region with nonzero vorticity. Vortices can help keep a flow attached or isolate a floor from side leakage. They also require kinetic energy and can strengthen mixing losses. “Remove all vortices” and “add a vortex” are both incomplete design instructions.

In a simple steady far-wake momentum analysis, drag relates to the momentum deficit integrated over a suitably chosen control surface, with pressure, transverse flow, and other terms treated consistently. Looking at one velocity slice does not supply all those terms. Near the car, pressure gradients and lateral velocities are especially important. A wake image is excellent for locating candidate problems but cannot replace a force integration.

Lift-related drag is a separate physical cost

A finite-span lifting device generates induced flow. Its aerodynamic force is tilted and has a drag component associated with producing lift or downforce. For an isolated attached wing, lifting-line models give Cdi ≈ Cl²/(πeAR). The square means downward lift has the same induced-drag sign as upward lift. This model does not capture all profile drag, supports, interactions, or ground effect. A diffuser can also affect the energy and momentum of the wake; it is not exempt from an energy cost simply because it is hidden beneath the car.

Changing frontal area, wheel exposure, mirror shape, cooling inlet/outlet placement, underfloor roughness, or rear taper may offer better drag reduction than reducing wing angle. Start by measuring the whole-car force and locating which flow regions change. A large drag contribution on a part is not necessarily removable without affecting the rest of the car. Delete a mirror in a simulation and you change more than its standalone projected area.

The right comparison controls

Use identical speed, density, reporting area, ride height, yaw, road motion, wheel treatment, domain, and numerical effort. Save shared colour scales and the same camera. Compare the force history before choosing a favourite flow image. If the baseline has not settled and the variant has, their final numbers can reflect sampling differences. Small drag gains need more scrutiny than spectacular-looking pictures.

Day 1 / 06 / Foundations

How to read a flow visualisation

You will choose a view that answers a question and avoid common mistakes with colours, smoke, and histories.

Pressure: always ask about the normal

A pressure map tells you how local pressure differs across the surface. It does not directly display downforce. On horizontal top and bottom faces the force directions are opposite. On a steep diffuser ramp, pressure force has both vertical and streamwise components. A large negative-Cp area may create suction with a substantial drag component, depending on its slope. For a device comparison, use the same Cp range and inspect the whole body as well as the device.

A Cp colour is nondimensional; a pascal colour depends on density and speed. Increasing speed can intensify a pressure-in-pascals plot even if the coefficients stay the same. Independent autoscaling can make a weak and strong pressure field look identical. A narrow extreme on a small area can dominate a legend while contributing little to the integrated load. Note whether a plotted field is a final snapshot, an average, or a reconstruction of averaged quantities.

Lines are not all the same

A streamline is tangent to the velocity field at an instant or to a chosen mean field. A pathline traces one particle through time. A streakline connects particles released from a source over time. They coincide in steady flow, but not generally in unsteady flow. EasyCFD's steady-field streamlines and smoke are primarily visualisations of its saved field. Solver iteration evolution and locally stepped pseudo-time are not a time-accurate physical film of your car's flow.

Move the seed rake. A few lines above a wing can miss its underside and tip vortices. Seeds inside solids, near poorly sampled cells, or in a clipped plane can disappear without proving a physical blockage. Inspect several heights and lateral positions. Read the legend and the camera's coordinate frame. Use vertical planes for roof and floor attachment, horizontal planes for side leakage, and cross-sections for wheel wakes and diffuser outflow.

Total pressure, turbulent kinetic energy, and surface flow

A low-total-pressure wake indicates loss of useful mechanical energy relative to the freestream, whereas a low static pressure can be part of efficient reversible acceleration. A turbulent-kinetic-energy map describes the modeled or resolved fluctuation scale appropriate to the solver, not a universal “bad flow” indicator. The k-ω SST model predicts statistics of turbulence; it does not show every real eddy. Surface flow and wall stress can suggest attachment and crossflow, but only to the fidelity supported by wall sampling and geometry resolution.

Force histories deserve the same care as flow images. A steadily falling residual measures how well the discretised equations are being solved; it does not establish that the chosen model or mesh matches reality. A flat Cd trace can coexist with changing Cl or pitching moment. Report the averaging window and compare its two halves. When an oscillation remains, extend the sample and quantify the mean's stability. The EasyCFD ± band is a spread of sub-window means, not a statistical 95% confidence interval and not total aerodynamic uncertainty.

Worked example

Five floor strips each have 0.20 m² area and mean downward pressure differences of 100, 300, 500, 400, and 200 Pa. The approximate floor load is 0.20 × 1500 = 300 N. A 900 Pa spot over only 0.005 m² contributes 4.5 N. Areas and normals matter more than the brightest pixel.

Day 2 / 07 / Devices

Wings: section shape, incidence, and stall

You will distinguish section geometry from effective incidence and recognise the limits of a simple wing calculation.

An inverted airfoil is a pressure device

A race-car wing is shaped and oriented to create lower pressure mainly on its lower surface and higher pressure mainly on its upper surface. Camber is the curvature of its mean line; thickness describes its envelope. Chord is the leading-edge-to-trailing-edge distance and span is the lateral extent. A symmetric section can make downforce at incidence; camber shifts its zero-lift angle. The integrated difference between upper and lower pressure is the main lift contribution for an ordinary attached wing, while shear and pressure recovery contribute drag.

The nominal mounting angle is measured relative to a reference on the car. The effective angle is measured relative to the local incoming air. A wing behind a sloping rear screen may see inclined, slower, turbulent air. Changing wing height can change local speed, incidence, and wake interaction at once. If local speed is 80% of freestream, local dynamic pressure is 64% of freestream. A coefficient derived in clean air at the freestream speed will overstate the load if applied unchanged to that slower flow.

Start with a linear model, then locate its boundary

For an ideal thin two-dimensional airfoil in attached incompressible flow, the lift slope is approximately 2π per radian relative to zero-lift incidence. A finite wing's slope is smaller because induced flow changes the effective angle. In the workbench below, define a positive downforce incidence α and an assumed zero-downforce incidence α₀. It estimates the magnitude coefficient CN = a(α - α₀), then profile and induced drag. This deliberately omits separation, body interference, endplates, ground effect, transition, and supports.

a ≈ 2π / [1 + 2/(e AR)] per radian
CN,wing ≈ a(α - α₀)   ;   CD,wing ≈ CD0 + CN,wing²/(πeAR)

Stall is a flow-state change

Increasing incidence makes the pressure recovery on the suction surface more demanding. Separation can grow and reduce lift slope, produce a plateau, or cause a sharp loss of downforce. Drag usually increases and the pressure centre may move. There is no universal automotive stall angle. Reynolds number, leading-edge geometry, roughness, upstream turbulence, ground proximity, and finite-span interactions all matter. A smoothly rising linear calculator cannot tell you when your installed wing stalls.

A multi-element wing uses gaps and carefully arranged elements to make stronger total turning while managing the boundary layers and pressure recovery of each element. The gap and overlap are aerodynamic parameters. Arbitrarily stacking two wings does not reproduce a designed slot. If the gap is unresolved by the mesh, a solver may effectively fuse or block it. The additional downforce can come with more drag and sensitivity, so compare the complete package through its actual operating range.

A practical first study

Keep section, span, chord, endplates, position, and supports fixed while testing a small sequence of mounting incidences. Inspect the incoming flow ahead of the wing, pressure recovery on the lower surface, spanwise load, and the whole-car moment. If downforce stops rising, investigate attachment and convergence before assuming a physical stall. Repeat at another mesh resolution and compare against a suitable experiment or qualified solver. EasyCFD's documented browser-wing ranking limitations make this last step especially important.

Day 2 / 08 / Devices

Finite span, endplates, Gurneys, and mounts

You will recognise three-dimensional wing costs and treat the installation as part of the device.

The span changes the price of downforce

For a rectangular wing, planform area S = bc and aspect ratio AR = b²/S = b/c. At the same wing coefficient, an isolated wing's induced-drag coefficient decreases with increasing aspect ratio. At a fixed required dimensional load, ideal induced drag can be written Di = N²/(πe q b²). This reveals why span matters: for fixed load, q, and efficiency factor, more span reduces the ideal induced penalty. It does not mean an arbitrarily wider wing is feasible or better in the installed car's flow.

Width restrictions, visibility, structural stiffness, roof wakes, yaw, and the tyre track create practical limits. Spanwise loading near a wheel wake may be worse than clean-air theory predicts. A wing may have nearly uniform section geometry but a very nonuniform pressure distribution. Look at several sections and integrated spanwise load, not just the centreline. An efficiency factor e is a modeling summary of loading, not a universal constant you can read from a wing's appearance.

Endplates constrain leakage; they also have drag

An endplate can impede flow from the high-pressure side to the low-pressure side around the tip, alter the loading, and help manage tip flow. It is not an infinite-span wall. Its size, overlap with the wing, proximity to body surfaces, shape, and yaw all matter. Extra wetted area and exposed frontal area contribute drag, and in crosswind it becomes a side-force surface with a moment arm. Compare the wing plus endplates plus supports, including changes to the car wake.

A Gurney flap is a small tab near the trailing edge, typically on the pressure side of a downforce wing. It can increase turning and change the rear pressure field, raising load and commonly increasing drag. The relevant size is h/c, not merely millimetres. A 5 mm tab on a 250 mm chord is 2% chord. Whether that is useful depends on the section and conditions. A coarse zero-thickness representation may not capture the small recirculating structure behind the tab.

Supports and real load paths

Supports that disturb a suction surface can degrade the wing's useful pressure recovery. Upper “swan-neck” arrangements move some attachments away from the lower suction surface, but their own shape, stiffness, and location still affect drag and loading. A mount in clean upper flow may create a different penalty from one in a body wake. Avoid treating a style of support as automatically superior without a controlled installed comparison.

The aerodynamic force must reach the chassis through a credible load path. A 300 N wing result at 100 km/h becomes about 1200 N at 200 km/h under constant coefficients, before transient factors and structural margins. Mounting deflection changes incidence and can alter the aero load itself. A flexible boot lid may change both geometry and load distribution. The teaching models omit wing mounts intentionally; that omission makes them suitable for mechanism exercises, not construction qualification.

Position and moment can dominate the result

A wing behind the rear axle adds rear load and can unload the front through its moment. Raising the wing can change inflow and the moment produced by drag acting above the road. A taller wing with more total downforce can therefore make the car's front share worse. Study load location, vertical height, and pitch moment as well as total force. Vehicle balance is covered rigorously in Day 4.

Worked example

For N = 500 N, q = 600 Pa, b = 1.6 m, and e = 0.8, ideal induced drag is 64.8 N. At b = 1.9 m it is 45.9 N if load and e stay fixed. This 18.9 N saving excludes profile drag, endplates, supports, and changes in local inflow. Do not present it as a measured whole-car gain.

Check: does reducing chord always improve an installed wing because AR rises?

No. At fixed span and required load, ideal induced drag depends on span, while changing chord alters section coefficient, Reynolds number, profile drag, stall margin, and structure. AR alone is not an installed design decision.

Day 2 / 09 / Devices

Splitters, air dams, and front load

You will explain a splitter's pressure difference and distinguish its function from an air dam and a decorative lip.

The front flow has choices

At the nose, approaching air divides over, under, and around the car and through open cooling passages. A splitter is a roughly horizontal surface extending near the lower front edge. High pressure on its upper side and lower pressure beneath it can generate downward load. Its leading edge, connection to the floor, side leakage, ground clearance, and the nearby wheel flow determine the actual distribution. The front fascia's stagnation region and cooling inlet cannot be treated as separate from this flow split.

An air dam is primarily a vertical barrier that reduces or redirects underbody inflow. It can reduce a messy underbody's drag or change lift, but also adds pressure drag itself. A splitter deliberately offers horizontal area on which a pressure difference can act. A lip may combine both effects depending on shape. Product names do not define the physics; inspect the surfaces, the flow path, and the resulting pressures.

A sensible first load estimate

If a 0.35 m² splitter experiences a mean pressure difference of 350 Pa downward, the estimated load is 122.5 N. This is a bookkeeping example, not a way to prescribe the pressure difference. Increasing overhang increases available area but can change the field, front-wheel interaction, bending, and ground-contact risk. You cannot assume load rises linearly with overhang once the flow or geometry changes.

Clearance can be crucial. A smaller gap may increase acceleration and suction, but it can also reduce mass flow, increase leakage sensitivity, or create intermittent blockage. Braking pitch, bumps, kerbs, and aerodynamic suspension compression all reduce the available gap. A rigid static simulation with a perfectly smooth road misses those operating conditions. State the loaded clearance, not just the dimension measured while parked.

The floor connection matters

A splitter ending at a rough open underside may not feed a stable floor flow. A smooth tray can reduce disturbance and interact with the rear diffuser. Sealing the top side against high-pressure leakage changes the pressure difference; adding a side fence changes three-dimensional flow. A front modification can starve a rear device or improve its inflow, so whole-car force and axle balance must be measured. The drag of the splitter group alone cannot represent that system change.

A splitter ahead of the front axle has a large front-loading moment arm. That may be useful, but the rear equivalent load can decrease for a given point load. Also account for drag at its height and any rear aerodynamic changes. Its load path may pass through bumper skins, brackets, and chassis mounts. The pressure distribution affects bending: two splitters with the same total force can have different peak bending moments and support needs.

Test a mechanism, not a photograph

For a first study keep width, leading-edge shape, floor connection, and position fixed. Compare a baseline and one overhang. Examine upper/lower Cp, front-wheel cross-sections, floor mass-flow behaviour, and axle load. Then sweep clearance. If the geometry looks good but the computed splitter force is negligible, determine whether the app represented its thickness and leading edge adequately before discarding the concept.

Day 2 / 10 / Devices

Canards, vortices, and wheel-flow control

You will separate a small device's direct force from its effect on downstream surfaces.

Small surfaces can change a large flow system

Automotive canards, or dive planes, are small inclined surfaces near the front corners. They can contribute direct downforce and generate vortical flow that changes side leakage, wheel wakes, or downstream attachment. A modest direct device load can accompany a larger body change. The reverse also occurs: a useful device force can be outweighed by a detrimental downstream change. Evaluate the difference on the whole car and retain the group breakdown as an explanation.

Pressure tends to equalise around a free edge, causing spanwise motion and a rolled-up vortex. A vortex may draw higher-momentum air into a boundary layer or create a pressure barrier to leakage. These are hypotheses about a specific installation. The vortex trajectory depends on local flow, yaw, position, other vortices, and the wheel. A hand-drawn line from a canard directly to a diffuser is not evidence that the real vortex reaches that location intact.

The rotating wheel is an active aerodynamic obstacle

A wheel rotates while translating with the car. In a car-fixed model its centre is fixed and its surface velocity varies around the circumference. At the nominal road contact the surface velocity approaches the road's velocity. Tyre shoulder shape, wheelhouse openings, steering, rim ventilation, contact-patch treatment, and the road gap all influence the wake. A smooth cylinder is a simplified teaching model, not a real tyre.

Wheel deflectors can redirect approaching flow, but a change in front-wheel flow can alter floor suction and rear-wheel conditions. Covering a wheel can reduce one drag source while changing cooling or force balance. A coarse representation of the contact patch may create an artificial jet through a gap or excessive blockage. The supplied pack uses cylindrical wheels with a deliberate 10 mm road gap to suit the app's geometric assumptions. That gap is a numerical exercise condition, not a recommendation for a physical tyre.

How to observe a vortex responsibly

Use cross-sections at several downstream stations with velocity vectors or appropriate vortex diagnostics, and track surface pressure and integrated force changes. Turbulent kinetic energy alone is not a vortex detector. A single vorticity-magnitude contour can light up a shear layer as well as a coherent vortex. If using Q-criterion or another diagnostic, record its threshold and sign convention. Test whether the conclusion survives a changed threshold, a finer mesh, and small yaw.

Control canard angle, area, position, and count separately at first. Adding several surfaces at once prevents attribution. Because these devices are small, geometry resolution and wall modeling are critical. A canard represented by very few cells may still appear smooth in the renderer. Real improvement must exceed the numerical sensitivity and be confirmed in relevant conditions. The current browser solver is especially exploratory for small downforce devices.

When the direct-device result misleads

Suppose canards produce 40 N direct downforce and 12 N drag. The body/floor change adds 25 N downforce and removes 5 N drag; total improvement is 65 N downforce for 7 N added drag. Another installation may give the same direct 40 N but unload the floor by 60 N; then the package loses 20 N downforce. This is why a device-only efficiency rating cannot decide a complete car modification.

Check: a canard's downstream smoke trail is visible. Does that prove it seals the underfloor?

No. Trace the relevant pressure and crossflow changes and demonstrate the whole-car force effect. A visible trail does not establish leakage suppression or useful load.

Day 2 / 11 / Devices

Floors and diffusers: acceleration and recovery

You will explain the complete underfloor path and distinguish ideal recovery from a prediction of downforce.

The diffuser is the rear part of a connected passage

A low floor passage can accelerate air and establish low static pressure beneath the car. A rear diffuser increases the passage height toward the exit, allowing the flow to decelerate and recover pressure. Successful recovery helps the system sustain useful underfloor flow and suction upstream. The downward load commonly comes from a broad floor area as well as the ramp. Calling the diffuser a device that “sucks air out” skips the pressure and mass-flow solution that you need to understand.

The upstream entry, throat height, ramp length, side leakage, wheel wakes, exit location, and rear wake all contribute. A ramp bolted under an obstructed suspension is not equivalent to a coherent floor feeding the same ramp. The exit does not necessarily see freestream static pressure. A rear wing can change the pressure above and behind the diffuser, altering its flow. That is a possible interaction to test, not guaranteed free downforce.

A limited one-dimensional recovery model

For a constant-width passage with throat height h₁ and exit height h₂ = h₁ + ℓ tan θ, the geometric area ratio R = h₂/h₁. If the mass flow is prescribed and the flow is uniform and attached, continuity gives U₂ = U₁/R. Ideal Bernoulli recovery gives p₂ - p₁ = ½ρU₁²(1 - 1/R²). This is a maximum ideal recovery for that assumed passage flow, not a whole-car downforce calculation. It cannot tell you how much air enters, where separation begins, or what pressure acts over the floor.

Workbench · Diffuser geometry and ideal recovery

Teaching model: fixed 35 m/s throat flow, constant width, no friction, leakage, or separation. Changing the ramp updates geometry and an ideal recovery only. There is deliberately no predicted car downforce.

Angle alone is not a design rule

A longer ramp can attain a given area ratio with a smaller angle, but packaging and friction may change. A steeper ramp can strengthen the adverse pressure gradient and promote separation. Fences can alter crossflow and vortices; their effects depend on floor conditions and can add drag. Widthwise expansion produces another recovery demand. Compare throat height, ramp length, expansion, and upstream state together rather than declaring every 7° diffuser safe or every 14° diffuser stalled.

Near the ground, decreasing ride height can first increase load and later reduce it as the flow loses attachment or supply. The transition can be asymmetric and abrupt. A symmetric half-car steady model can suppress possible left/right states. Experiments on one diffuser-equipped bluff body demonstrate such low-clearance load reduction, but their exact heights and ramp geometry are not transferable as a universal operating map.

The teaching kit uses replacement bodies

The two supplied diffuser shapes are built into alternative body meshes. They remove material that the flat-floor shell otherwise occupies; simply adding a ramp to the baseline shell would leave the original floor blocking the intended passage. Use exactly one body group at a time, while keeping all body part switches on for a shared grid. Inspect the underside before every run. This simple distinction between replacing geometry and adding geometry is essential in real CAD studies.

Day 2 / 12 / Devices

Ride height, skirts, rake, and cooling

You will identify the conditions that can make a promising floor fragile or incomplete.

Sealing is a pressure-management problem

When underfloor pressure is below the adjacent air, lateral leakage can weaken suction and alter the floor flow. A skirt or fence can impede that leakage, guide flow, or generate a useful vortex. It also adds wetted area and can change wheel interactions. Mechanical skirts close to the road risk contact and must operate over suspension travel and road disturbances. A vortex-based seal depends on the flow maintaining its position and strength. Neither is immune to yaw or changes in clearance.

The supplied paper investigates diagonal sealing skirts as part of its particular floor. Those are different from a generic vertical side skirt or a side sill extension. The course pack uses two simple side fences as a teaching variation. Their results cannot claim to reproduce the paper's diagonal-skirt performance. Read the geometry specification before comparing a device name across studies.

Ride height and rake form an operating map

Front and rear ride heights define a floor's pose. Rake is the longitudinal inclination; for small angles it is approximately (hrear - hfront)/wheelbase. Changing rake changes the entry gap, underfloor expansion, diffuser relative angle, body incidence, and wing incidence together. It is not merely a diffuser-angle adjustment. An aero map records forces and moments across these conditions so the suspension can operate within a stable range.

A useful map might start with front/rear heights of 80, 110, and 140 mm for an imagined car, but those are study parameters that must fit its geometry and contact constraints. Do not translate the entire car down until a tyre penetrates the road. In the browser app a road-height adjustment represents a global geometric shift, not a suspension model with wheel centres fixed and the sprung body pitching. Correct pose studies require carefully prepared geometry variants or a dedicated pose tool.

Cooling flow belongs in the drag and lift budget

Air passing through a radiator loses total pressure. Inlets and outlets also change the outer body pressure field. Closing a grille can reduce drag in a geometry model but fail the thermal duty. A hood outlet may reduce engine-bay pressure and front lift when correctly placed; it can also disrupt windshield flow or worsen drag. You need mass flow, pressure drop, heat rejection, and the external flow interaction to make a cooling decision.

For a first-order porous model, Δp may be represented as K₁U + K₂U² with coefficients fitted to a core's measured pressure drop. Couple that with appropriate inlet and outlet passages and a thermal requirement. An external-only sealed-body simulation cannot assess radiator airflow. The present course therefore treats cooling as a missing physical subsystem when the geometry contains no ducts or core. The cooling-flow feature request specifies the additional outputs needed to study this tradeoff.

End-of-day package audit

By now each device should have a mechanism, a likely cost, and an operating sensitivity in your notebook. For a wing: local incidence, profile and induced drag, and structural deflection. For a splitter: pressure difference, contact risk, and front balance. For canards: direct load, downstream interaction, and yaw sensitivity. For a floor/diffuser: leakage, pressure recovery, and clearance dependence. For skirts: sealing, drag, and a physically viable gap. If your notes only say “more downforce,” add the mechanism and the test that could disprove it.

Day 2 checkpoint: why is a sealed grille or a 5 mm skirt gap not automatically a valid improvement?

A sealed grille changes thermal capability, while a tiny skirt gap may disappear under suspension motion or be unresolved numerically. A useful package must meet cooling, clearance, structural, and aerodynamic requirements together.

What was actually studied?

The authors used a self-designed road-car geometry about 4 m long with approximately 2.45 m wheelbase. The configurations were a flat-underfloor baseline, a rear 7° diffuser, that diffuser with diagonal sealing skirts, and a diffuser configuration with front slots near the tyres. The front-slot case is a separate branch; it should not be read as “skirts plus front slots.” Their numerical method was steady incompressible RANS in OpenFOAM with simpleFoam and k-ω SST. The study used a tetrahedral mesh with local refinements and boundary layers; it is not the same mesh pipeline as EasyCFD's Cartesian browser solver or its snappyHexMesh backend.

The inlet and moving ground were set to 25 m/s, equivalent to 90 km/h. Wheels used rotating-wall conditions at 87 rad/s. Only half the car was simulated with symmetry; remaining side/top boundaries used slip. The finest mesh had 6.7 million elements. Convergence was judged using residuals below 10^-5 and stabilised coefficients at around 1000 iterations. Each of these conditions limits what can be inferred about another setup.

The mesh description reports nine wall layers with a growth factor of 1.5, a first-cell height of 0.01 mm, and an averaged y+ of 40. These are reported quantities, not values measured by this course. Day 3 / 14 / Methods

What the CFD solver actually solves

You will understand the modeling hierarchy sufficiently to choose an appropriate question and interpret solver output.

Mass and momentum are the foundation

For an incompressible Newtonian fluid, continuity requires ∇·U = 0. Momentum balances acceleration with pressure forces and viscous stresses. At high Reynolds number, a direct simulation of all turbulent scales around a full car is extremely expensive. Engineering CFD normally models at least part of turbulence. The important distinction is what the chosen method resolves, what it averages, and what its additional closures assume.

∇ · U = 0
ρ[∂U/∂t + (U · ∇)U] = -∇p + ∇ · τ
RANS adds modeled stresses from unresolved velocity fluctuations.

Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes uses a mean velocity and additional stresses from turbulent fluctuations. An eddy-viscosity model approximates those stresses using the local mean strain and a turbulence viscosity. k-ω SST transports turbulent kinetic energy k and a specific dissipation variable ω, combining near-wall and outer-flow behaviour through blending functions. It is a useful engineering closure, not a guarantee of correct separated flow for every car.

Numerical convergence and physical accuracy are different

A discretisation replaces the continuous equations by algebraic relations on a mesh. Pressure-velocity coupling enforces mass conservation while momentum is advanced or iterated. Linear-solver residuals, nonlinear residuals, and force settling are related but distinct diagnostics. You can solve a poor mesh or inappropriate model very consistently. Refining the mesh reduces a class of numerical errors but does not remove a missing cooling passage, an incorrect road boundary, or a turbulence-model bias.

Steady RANS seeks a stationary mean solution. Local time stepping can accelerate convergence by giving different cells different effective step sizes. That progression is pseudo-time, not a physical time record. URANS uses time-dependent averaged equations; LES resolves a larger share of unsteady structures while modeling smaller scales. These approaches require appropriate temporal and spatial resolution and sampling. Switching labels to “transient” does not establish a validated unsteady-load prediction.

Two EasyCFD engines, two numerical treatments

The browser solver uses a stretched Cartesian cut-cell formulation, thin-part handling, SST turbulence, and wall modeling. The OpenFOAM path uses the existing backend's snappyHexMesh/prism-layer/simpleFoam pipeline. Their discrete operators, mesh geometry, wall representation, sampling, and convergence can differ even when the broad physical model and inputs sound alike. Agreement between two unqualified solvers is reassuring evidence about a comparison, but it is not experimental validation. Disagreement does not tell you which is physically right without further evidence.

The repository documents significant uncertainty for lift and small aero devices, including cases where browser wing downforce is under-predicted or the incidence ordering differs from OpenFOAM. Fast, Medium, and Precise are effort presets rather than accuracy certifications. The two-grid averaging in the browser Precise preset is not a substitute for three independent converged meshes and an observed-order study. Use Custom cases and export histories for a deliberate refinement investigation.

State a prediction's scope before running it

An external incompressible rigid-car calculation at one speed and zero yaw can answer a narrow question about that modeled condition. It cannot establish thermal adequacy, body motion over bumps, tyre deformation, structural flexibility, acoustics, or gust response unless those mechanisms are included and qualified. A responsible scope statement is practical: it prevents you from spending time resolving detail that cannot answer your actual design question.

Check: your residuals are low and the fields look smooth. Have you validated the diffuser?

No. You have evidence about iterative behaviour. You still need force/moment settling, mesh and domain sensitivity, appropriate physical assumptions, and comparison with suitable experimental evidence.

Geometry, boundaries, and the virtual tunnel

You will prepare a controlled external-flow case and identify boundary-condition mistakes before solving.

Geometry is an engineering input

Check dimensions immediately after importing. STL does not encode units, so a millimetre model interpreted as metres can invalidate every setting. Confirm the nose and up axes, body/wheel identification, wheel radius, clearance, missing faces, duplicate shells, and the enabled groups. Keep variants in one common coordinate frame. Export a stable naming scheme and record which surfaces were removed or replaced. A diffuser ramp inside an unchanged closed body is not the intended geometry.

Closed, outward-oriented volumes are generally easier to interpret than fragmentary surfaces. Thin parts may be handled as zero-thickness walls by the browser solver, which is a numerical representation rather than a full resolution of thickness and edge structure. Very small gaps, slotted elements, and internal channels need explicit fidelity checks. Geometry-rendering quality and mesh quality are separate. Merging or sealing a mesh can alter slots and tyre gaps; review the resulting geometry before running.

Watertightness and orientation are separate checks. Two triangles can meet along an edge while both traverse it in the same direction, leaving one face reversed. Inspect consistent adjacency and outward orientation per closed component, not only the absence of holes. The course generator verifies finite vertices, two opposite directed incidences per edge, and positive signed volume, and records file hashes. The app's current undirected open-edge count does not provide all of those orientation checks.

Use the road frame deliberately

At zero yaw in a car-fixed frame the road moves rearward with the road-relative speed, and the tyre angular velocity should match rolling approximately through ω = Uroad/R. The body's no-slip velocity is zero. A stationary floor creates a different floor boundary layer from a moving road. Non-rotating wheels also change the flow. These can be useful controlled sensitivity experiments, but they do not represent a car driving over a road.

Crosswind complicates this frame: road motion and wheel rotation are set by vehicle motion over the road, while inlet direction and magnitude are set by air motion relative to the car. A lateral wind does not make the road slide sideways. Audit the solver's implementation when interpreting yaw cases; use the wind triangle in Lesson 22 to separate road speed and airspeed. An app with only one speed field cannot automatically express every ambient-wind condition.

Keep artificial boundaries far enough away

A velocity inlet prescribes approaching flow; a pressure outlet allows the downstream pressure level to anchor the solution. Side/top boundaries and the ground influence the whole pressure field if too close. Blockage is projected frontal area divided by the tunnel cross-section. A small blockage ratio helps, but a single threshold cannot certify negligible confinement. The rear wake needs sufficient length for outflow conditions to avoid distorting the region of interest.

As a starting experiment, consider several car lengths upstream, more downstream, and generous lateral/top clearance; the necessary values depend on boundaries and the question. Expand the domain and compare forces and moments while maintaining comparable near-car resolution. If the domain changes the result more than the modification does, the design ranking is not resolved. Record actual domain bounds rather than only a vague phrase like “large tunnel.”

Fixed conditions make variants comparable

Use an explicitly declared density, freestream turbulence model/input, reference area, road and wheel treatment, orientation, and pose. Keep the complete variant family present in the shared-grid design. A group switch turns a geometry variant on for the solve while allowing inactive variants to keep shaping the common grid. Turning the part's own switch off removes it from that grid definition. Check the saved grid identifier instead of assuming two runs used identical cells.

Day 3 / 16 / Methods

Meshes, y+, convergence, and uncertainty

You will build a verification argument and avoid reporting a precise-looking number with unjustified precision.

Refine where the relevant gradients live

Leading edges, thin wings, floor gaps, diffuser ramps, wheel contact regions, and separated shear layers need appropriate resolution. A global cell count alone does not describe that resolution. Report chordwise/spanwise sampling, near-wall layers, smallest effective gap representation, and wake refinement. On a cut-cell grid, small shifts of the geometry relative to cells can alter integration and geometry representation. Paired studies on a shared grid reduce one comparison difference but do not prove the design delta is mesh independent.

Near-wall y+ depends on the solution

Define y+ = uτy/ν with friction velocity uτ = √(|τwall|/ρ) and y the representative wall-normal distance of the first cell point. A wall-resolved treatment and a wall-function treatment have different requirements. Order-one y+ is a typical goal for a genuinely wall-resolved approach; log-layer wall functions generally need their appropriate log-layer range and adequate layer coverage. Blended wall models are not permission to ignore near-wall resolution. Values vary over the car and require inspection, not just a target entered before solving.

Use the distance convention from the solver. If a prism's full height is used instead of its centre-to-wall distance, an estimate can differ by about a factor of two. Check actual y+ distributions on the wing, floor, and ramp, not only the global average. Near separation τwall tends toward zero, so a small y+ there alone does not demonstrate excellent boundary-layer resolution. Layer collapse and insufficient layers can remain serious problems.

Three levels of convergence

First, iterative convergence: algebraic and nonlinear residuals and mass conservation must be suitable for the case. Second, force/moment convergence: means and trends must be stable over a justified window, or a physically appropriate unsteady average must be sampled. Third, discretisation convergence: repeat on finer, comparable grids. Also test domain sensitivity. Each test addresses a different uncertainty source. Their absence cannot be compensated by an extra decimal place.

A narrow but useful three-grid calculation

For geometrically similar grids with a common spacing ratio r > 1, label φ₁ fine, φ₂ medium, φ₃ coarse. For a monotonic asymptotic sequence, p = ln|(φ₃-φ₂)/(φ₂-φ₁)|/ln r. Richardson extrapolation gives φext = φ₁ + (φ₁-φ₂)/(rᵖ-1). A common fine-grid GCI is 1.25 |(φ₁-φ₂)/φ₁|/(rᵖ-1). This factor assumes a three-grid analysis and is a reporting convention, not a universal statistical confidence level.

Workbench · Three-grid verification arithmetic

Teaching calculation for constant spacing ratio, monotonic converging data, and a nonzero metric. Invalid sequences are rejected. The result estimates discretisation uncertainty under these assumptions, not model or experimental uncertainty.

If differences change sign, grow with refinement, vanish through rounding, or involve incompatible meshes, do not force the formula. Lift near zero requires an absolute uncertainty scale rather than a misleading relative percentage. Local/adaptive refinement and a two-grid averaged preset do not automatically provide the necessary constant-r sequence. Analyse drag, lift, moment, and design differences independently. A grid study on baseline Cd does not certify the wing's Cl.

How small a design difference can you trust?

Suppose ΔCd = -0.004 but plausible variations from force sampling and grid phase are of order 0.010. You have not resolved that gain. Paired differences can share correlated error, but assuming cancellation without evidence is optimistic. Repeat the comparison across mesh levels and reasonable shifts, quantify the delta itself, and use a physically validated reference when needed. The current app's temporal bands describe window behaviour only; they do not include geometry, mesh, boundary, turbulence-model, or measurement uncertainty.

Day 4 / 19 / Vehicle integration

Pitching moment, axle load, and tyre grip

You will derive equivalent aerodynamic axle loads and distinguish those loads from a complete handling prediction.

The location of the load matters

A car with 800 N downforce concentrated at the rear behaves differently from one with the same total split evenly. To calculate equivalent front/rear aero loads, use the total vertical force and the pitching moment about a declared reference. This is a static force-and-moment decomposition. The loads describe the aerodynamic increment that the axles must balance; they are not necessarily the pressure integrals on the front and rear halves of the body.

Let s be distance aft of the front axle and ℓ the wheelbase. Let N be positive downward load. Define Mload about the front axle as the moment that produces positive rear equivalent load. For vertical point loads Mload = Σ sᵢNᵢ; include any additional aerodynamic couple with the same sign convention. Then Nrear = Mload/ℓ and Nfront = Ntotal - Nrear. In EasyCFD's +X-aft, +Z-up frame, the appropriate Y moment about a road-level front-axle origin also includes hD from drag at height h. Always transform a saved moment to the chosen origin before applying the axle calculation.

Workbench · Two vertical point loads and axle balance

Teaching statics: splitter and wing vertical point loads only, no drag-height moment, aerodynamic couple, suspension deflection, braking, or lateral load transfer. Negative front aero load means aerodynamic unloading.

Worked example

Wheelbase is 2.6 m. A splitter adds 150 N at s = -0.4 m, and a wing adds 500 N at s = 3.0 m. Total N = 650 N, Mload = -60 + 1500 = 1440 N·m, Nrear = 553.8 N, and Nfront = 96.2 N. Without the splitter, the wing alone creates 576.9 N rear increment and -76.9 N front increment. More total downforce does not imply more front grip.

Front fraction is useful only with a meaningful total

Aero balance β = Nfront/Ntotal is commonly quoted as a percentage. If total downforce is near zero, the ratio becomes unstable. If one axle experiences lift, the fraction can be below 0% or above 100%; that is a warning about the physical load distribution, not a reason to clip the value. Report the signed axle increments as well as any percentage. Choose a desired balance from vehicle behaviour and tyre capability, not automatically from static mass distribution.

Add static weight, then include dynamics separately

For a 1100 kg car with 52% static front weight, total weight is 10,787 N; front static load is about 5610 N and rear about 5178 N. Add the equivalent aero increments at the evaluated speed to estimate quasi-static axle normal loads. Braking transfers approximately maₓhCG/ℓ between axles in a simple rigid-body model. Cornering also produces left/right transfer influenced by track width, suspension, roll stiffness, and geometry. Do not merge these mechanisms into a single “aero kilograms” number.

Tyres are load sensitive: lateral force capability generally grows less than proportionally with normal load over a practical range, and combined braking/cornering uses a shared traction capability. Extra downforce can help but does not give a proportional increase in cornering speed. Temperature, camber, pressure, slip angle, load transfer, and tyre construction all matter. Without an actual tyre model, course calculations establish load changes and hypotheses, not lap-time or handling guarantees.

Day 4 / 20 / Vehicle integration

Speed, power, braking, and circuit tradeoffs

You will quantify the power cost of drag and explain why a useful aero package depends on the circuit and operating speed.

The straight has a budget

In still air, aerodynamic power at the wheels is D × vehicle speed. For constant CdA it grows with U³. At 200 km/h, adding 100 N drag costs about 5.56 kW of wheel power. Powertrain efficiency determines the engine or electrical-input cost. Rolling resistance, gradients, drivetrain losses, and acceleration add further demands. Use the correct available wheel power and gearing rather than treating engine headline power as a direct aerodynamic budget.

A steady top-speed estimate equates available wheel power to aero power plus other losses at that speed. Ignoring other losses and assuming fixed power and coefficients gives Umax ∝ (P/CdA)^(1/3). A 10% increase in drag area therefore reduces this simplified top speed by about 3.1%. This is a sensitivity calculation, not a prediction of a specific car's terminal speed, because gearing and power curves can change the outcome.

Downforce helps where speed and tyre demand coincide

Aero load grows with airspeed squared if the coefficients remain constant. In a slow hairpin, mechanical grip may dominate; in a fast corner, downforce can materially increase normal load. A circuit with long flat-out straights can punish drag, while a circuit with many fast corners can reward downforce. That tradeoff depends on the car's power, tyres, mass, balance, and driver limits. The same package is not optimal everywhere.

For a highly simplified constant-μ tyre and an evenly balanced car, lateral acceleration capacity might scale like μ(mg + N)/m. Real tyres are load sensitive and axle-limited, so this formula often overstates the benefit. Even the simple model has speed coupling: N depends on the corner speed being solved for. An added wing that unloads the limiting front axle may give less benefit than its total downforce suggests.

Braking and pitch complicate the simple picture

Aerodynamic drag assists deceleration but is not a controllable tyre brake and falls with speed. Downforce can increase tyre braking capacity, yet longitudinal weight transfer and pitching motion change axle loads and floor clearances. A floor that is excellent in a level static pose can lose rear load in heavy braking or scrape at the front. A robust brake-zone package therefore needs aerodynamic pose data and a vehicle dynamics model or measured behaviour, not just a maximum-downforce setting.

Wind separates vehicle speed from airspeed. Drag force is set by the flow relative to the car; mechanical power expended by the moving car is the force component opposing vehicle motion times road speed. Do not multiply drag magnitude by total relative airspeed in an arbitrary crosswind and call it wheel power. At yaw, project the aerodynamic vector along the travel direction. The simple force calculator explicitly uses still air to avoid this ambiguity.

Use a transparent decision table

For each package list ΔCdA, front/rear downforce-area changes, operating range, mass, clearance cost, and uncertainty. Convert to forces at representative slow, medium, and fast speeds, then to still-air drag power. Check whether the useful load occurs at the speeds where the tyres need help. If a small drag saving is not resolved numerically, do not let a precise lap-time model turn it into a convincing fictional advantage.

Worked example

Take an illustrative package with ΔCdA = 0.05 m² and ΔCNA = 0.40 m². At 100 km/h it adds 23.6 N drag and 189 N downforce, costing 0.66 kW. At 200 km/h it adds 94.5 N drag and 756 N downforce, costing 5.25 kW. The incremental ratio is 8, but axle distribution and ride-height robustness still determine whether the package is useful.

Check: the package has the best downforce/drag ratio. Must it have the best lap time?

No. Absolute load, axle balance, speed distribution, power, tyre response, robustness, mass, and constraints also matter. A ratio is one descriptor, not a lap-time result.

Day 4 / 21 / Design method

Interactions, experiment design, and Pareto choices

You will quantify non-additivity and choose feasible candidates without relying on a single weighted score.

Devices interact through the shared flow field

A splitter can change floor entry. Skirts can alter underfloor leakage. A diffuser changes the rear wake. A rear wing can alter exit pressure and body separation. The complete package's change is therefore not generally the sum of isolated-device changes. Define an interaction effect for any metric Y using IAB = YAB - YA - YB + Y0, where 0 is baseline, A and B are separate modifications, and AB is their combination. Use the same physical and numerical conditions for all four cases.

For positive downforce, an illustrative baseline of 100 N, A of 180 N, B of 220 N, and AB of 340 N gives interaction 40 N. That is more load than the additive expectation of 300 N. But the 40 N matters only if the four-case comparison is resolved within uncertainty. Apply the same formula to drag, axle loads, and moments. A beneficial downforce interaction can accompany a harmful drag or balance interaction.

Screen factors, then study promising interactions

A one-factor-at-a-time sequence is easy to interpret but can miss interactions. A full two-level factorial study with k factors needs 2ᵏ cases: four factors produce sixteen configurations before repeats. Add a centre point when meaningful to detect curvature and repeat key cases to estimate numerical variation. A fractional design reduces runs but aliases some effects; document which interactions you cannot separate. Do not infer a nonlinear optimum from two levels alone.

Choose factors that correspond to a physical mechanism: wing incidence, floor entry clearance, skirt gap, diffuser geometry, or outlet position. Keep unrelated geometry unchanged. Define each level precisely in millimetres and degrees. Randomising physical test order helps avoid temperature, wind, and tyre-state drift; in deterministic CFD the analogous protection is consistent settings plus repeats across grids and numerical conditions. A response surface should be checked with independent cases before being used to optimise.

Multiple objectives lead to a frontier

A candidate is Pareto dominated if another feasible candidate has no worse drag and no less useful downforce, with at least one strict improvement. The nondominated set is a frontier of tradeoffs; it is not a single winner. Balance, cooling, clearance, load-path, and uncertainty constraints can eliminate an apparent aerodynamic winner. A package with more rear load but insufficient front load may be infeasible even when its total downforce is high.

A weighted score such as N - λD can select a point for a declared value of λ, but weights hide priorities and their units. Do not turn numerical noise into a highly precise optimum.

Day 4 / 22 / Vehicle integration

Yaw, pitch, and a robust aero map

You will design an operating envelope and distinguish road speed, relative wind, and geometric pose.

Crosswind is a vector problem

Let road-relative vehicle velocity be Vcar and ambient wind velocity be W in a common ground frame. Relative air velocity in the car frame is W - Vcar; its opposite convention can be used for oncoming wind if stated consistently. For a car travelling at 100 km/h with a perpendicular 5 m/s wind, the oncoming airspeed is √(27.78² + 5²) ≈ 28.22 m/s and yaw magnitude is atan(5/27.78) ≈ 10.2°. The road still moves directly rearward at 27.78 m/s in the car frame.

Side force, yawing moment, and rolling moment matter in this case. Endplates, exposed wheel faces, skirts, and asymmetric separation can create sensitivity that a zero-yaw study misses. Positive and negative yaw are not redundant for an asymmetric car, asymmetric cooling openings, or a numerically symmetry-constrained setup. Inspect both. A mean yawing moment alone does not define gust response without the vehicle dynamics and time-dependent aerodynamic behaviour.

Workbench · Relative wind triangle

Vector calculation for longitudinal headwind and lateral crosswind. The app's single speed/yaw input must be audited before claiming independent road and airspeed conditions.

In the current WebGPU setup, the speed field sets the longitudinal inlet component Ux and the road speed; yaw adds Uy = Ux tan(yaw). For example, 100 km/h with 10.2° yaw gives approximately 5 m/s lateral relative wind, while the road remains at 27.78 m/s. The resulting total airspeed is about 28.22 m/s. Check the saved freestream and dynamic pressure when comparing coefficients. This coupling can express pure crosswind, but an independent longitudinal headwind needs separate road and inlet speeds; that is part of the operating-map feature request.

Pose changes the complete installation

Pitch during braking moves the front splitter toward the road and changes the underfloor entry/rake. Rear squat changes diffuser clearance and wing/body incidence. Roll changes left/right floor and skirt gaps. Steering changes wheel exposure and wheelhouse flow. Suspension motion, tyre deflection, and chassis flexibility determine the actual pose. A rigid car translated vertically with rotating wheels fixed in the body is a different experiment from a sprung body moving relative to wheel centres.

Create pose variants in CAD with clear reference points and wheel-road geometry held appropriately. Record front and rear loaded ride height, pitch, roll, steering, and tyre treatment. A dedicated app tool for these states would help, but until it exists manual variants must be carefully inspected. Do not use the course's clearance slider as proof of a physically correct suspension-pitch study.

A modest first robustness matrix

Begin with the nominal case, ±5° and ±10° yaw, several feasible front/rear heights, and at least one braking-like and acceleration-like pose. Add combinations near suspected attachment transitions. If this becomes too costly, screen with the simpler solver and then qualify the promising and worst cases with an appropriate higher-fidelity method or test. Keep the actual road/air velocity convention recorded, especially under crosswind.

Report minimum useful front load, maximum drag cost, balance excursions, and sensitivity gradients across the envelope. A peak-load setting that falls off abruptly with a 10 mm height change may be worse than a slightly lower-load broad plateau. Make the objective robust performance under realistic variation. Its definition needs tolerances for geometry, alignment, ride height, wind, surface roughness, and measurement uncertainty.

Feedback can couple aero and suspension

More downforce compresses springs, changing ride height, which changes downforce again. A simple static coupling solves suspension displacement and aero load together; dynamic coupling also needs damping, mass, road input, and time-dependent forces. Strong height sensitivity can create problematic behaviour even when a rigid nominal case looks excellent. A static RANS sweep cannot establish porpoising frequency or stability. Identify the potential feedback and obtain the needed dynamics evidence.

Check: why might a nominally “best” low-clearance diffuser be a poor track choice?

It may lose load, shift balance, contact the road, or change flow state under realistic pitch, roll, yaw, and roughness. The worst relevant condition can matter more than the nominal peak.

Day 4 / 23 / Qualification

Physical validation and a buildable modification

You will plan useful measurements and separate aerodynamic evidence from structural and operational readiness.

A test must discriminate between hypotheses

Choose a measurement that can confirm or refute the proposed mechanism. Pressure taps can show whether floor suction and diffuser recovery changed. Tufts and oil-flow methods can reveal surface-flow direction and possible separation. A force balance can measure aerodynamic loads under controlled wind-tunnel conditions. Suspension loads or deflections can indicate on-car load changes when mechanical effects are modeled and calibrated. No single method measures everything.

A rolling-road wind tunnel helps reproduce road-relative motion, but tyre shape, contact patch, support interference, blockage, scale, and Reynolds number still require attention. A fixed-floor small model may be useful for qualitative screening while giving misleading underfloor loads. State those limits. When comparing to CFD, reproduce geometry, pose, velocity, turbulence, road/wheel treatment, and the test facility where relevant; otherwise the mismatch combines numerical and test-condition differences.

On-car tests need control and repetition

Coastdown can help estimate road-load changes, but wind, slope, tyre temperature/pressure, drivetrain losses, and speed measurement confound the aerodynamic component. Use an established calibrated protocol on an appropriate controlled facility. Downforce cannot be inferred uniquely from a quicker lap or a driver's confidence. Separate driver variability, tyre evolution, fuel mass, traffic, weather, and mechanical setup. A-B-A sequences and repeated conditions help reveal drift but do not remove all confounding.

Instrumented ride height and suspension deflection require spring, motion-ratio, bump-stop, and damping interpretation. Pressure taps need calibrated sensors, suitable tubing response, and known locations. If the vehicle is pitching or rolling, static deflection-to-load conversion is insufficient. A measurement's bandwidth and uncertainty must suit the proposed effect. A 10 N change is not meaningful if the total measurement chain cannot resolve it.

Structure and packaging are separate checks

Define an aerodynamic load envelope with speed, wind, pose, and uncertainty, then transfer it to structural analysis. Include realistic distribution, load paths, fastener loads, fatigue, impact/contact, material degradation, and allowable deflection. A CFD force integral does not prove a bracket, composite laminate, boot lid, or bumper can carry that load. A geometry with free-floating wings and omitted mounts cannot qualify a real build.

A splitter needs ground-contact and debris considerations. A floor needs heat, drainage, service access, and ventilation. A diffuser must accommodate exhaust, suspension, and structural members. A wing needs mounting stiffness and an acceptable deflection-to-incidence relationship. Endplates and canards may be subject to dimensional or competition constraints. Check the actual applicable requirements for the intended use before release; this course does not prescribe jurisdiction-specific road legality or a ruleset.

Make a release decision with an evidence ladder

Separate concept plausibility, geometry readiness, numerical verification, physical validation, structural readiness, and operational readiness. Passing one rung does not imply the next. A prototype can be approved for a limited instrumented test without being approved for unrestricted use. Record which conclusion each artifact supports and who reviews the remaining work. This is normal engineering traceability, not an obstacle to experimentation.

Worked example

A simulated wing adds 200 N at 100 km/h. Constant-coefficient scaling suggests 800 N at 200 km/h, but the structural input should also account for the relevant off-design aerodynamic envelope and uncertainty. “The simulation ran” is evidence of software execution; “the mount passed” requires an independent structural analysis and suitable test.

Check: you gained 0.8 s on one lap after fitting canards. Did you measure their aerodynamic benefit?

No. That observation combines many variables and does not isolate aero force or mechanism. Use controlled repeated comparison and targeted measurements to establish the change.

Memorising a preferred wing or diffuser angle is not enough.

Appendix A / Worked checks

Exercises with worked answers

Attempt these without the answers first. The numbers are teaching inputs, not fitted results for any particular vehicle. Show units, force signs, area convention, and assumptions in every calculation.

1. One speed, two reference areas

A run at 120 km/h has D = 550 N in air at 1.225 kg/m³. Calculate Cd using Aref = 2.0 m² and 2.3 m². What changed physically?

Worked answer

U = 33.333 m/s, q = 680.56 Pa. Cd is 550/(680.56 × 2.0) = 0.4041 or 550/(680.56 × 2.3) = 0.3514. The force did not change. The difference is only the reporting denominator. CdA = 0.8082 m² in both cases.

2. Added downforce while still lifting

Baseline Cl = +0.25; variant Cl = +0.10 at Aref = 2.2 m², 100 km/h. Calculate the added downforce and final lift.

Worked answer

qA = 472.61 × 2.2 = 1039.74 N. Added downforce = -(0.10 - 0.25) × 1039.74 = 155.96 N. Final lift remains +103.97 N. The variant improves vertical load but does not create net downforce.

3. Wing reporting convention

A wing has S = 0.50 m², Cl,wing = -1.2, Cd,wing = 0.15. Convert its contribution to car-area coefficients with Acar = 2.2 m², assuming the same q.

Worked answer

Cl,car contribution = -1.2 × 0.50/2.2 = -0.2727, Cd,car contribution = 0.15 × 0.50/2.2 = 0.03409. These are the wing's force contribution only, not the whole-car changes. Body interaction can change the final delta.

4. Scale-model Reynolds number

A 4 m car at 30 m/s has ν = 1.5 × 10^-5 m²/s. What Re does a 1 m model have at the same speed? What speed would match full-size Re in the same air?

Worked answer

Full size Re = 8 million; model Re = 2 million. Matching requires 120 m/s. At a ≈ 343 m/s that is Mach 0.35, so simply increasing speed creates a different compressibility regime and practical facility demands. Use a documented similarity strategy rather than claiming exact equivalence.

5. Ideal diffuser expansion

A constant-width ramp has h1 = 0.10 m, ℓ = 0.80 m, θ = 7°. Given prescribed U1 = 35 m/s and ρ = 1.225, compute ideal exit speed and pressure recovery.

Worked answer

h2 = 0.10 + 0.80 tan 7° ≈ 0.1982 m; R ≈ 1.982. U2 ≈ 17.66 m/s. q1 ≈ 750.31 Pa; ideal recovery ≈ 750.31(1 - 1/1.982²) ≈ 559 Pa. These values assume uniform attached flow and the prescribed mass flow. They do not predict floor pressure or car downforce.

6. Three-grid arithmetic

Independent comparable grids give fine Cd = 0.3000, medium = 0.3060, coarse = 0.3195 with spacing ratio r = 1.5. Calculate p, extrapolated Cd, and fine GCI.

Worked answer

Difference ratio = 0.0135/0.006 = 2.25, so p = ln(2.25)/ln(1.5) = 2. φext = 0.3000 - 0.006/1.25 = 0.2952. Fine GCI = 1.25(0.006/0.3000)/1.25 = 0.020 = 2.0%. This depends on monotonic asymptotic refinement and converged cases; it is not total physical uncertainty.

7. Aero couple without vertical force

A pure aerodynamic moment adds +260 N·m in the rear-loading convention about the front axle of a 2.6 m wheelbase car. Total vertical aerodynamic force is zero. What are the equivalent axle increments?

Worked answer

Nrear = 260/2.6 = +100 N, Nfront = -100 N. There is no net vertical aero load, but front is unloaded and rear is loaded. The front fraction of total downforce is undefined because the denominator is zero.

8. Drag at height

A rearward drag force of 200 N acts at height 0.8 m with no net vertical force. Using a road-level front-axle origin and the EasyCFD axes, what equivalent axle-load change follows for ℓ = 2.6 m?

Worked answer

The Y moment is hD = +160 N·m. Nrear = +61.54 N and Nfront = -61.54 N in the declared convention. This is part of aerodynamic force/moment decomposition. Actual vehicle equilibrium under propulsion/braking also includes those forces, their application heights, and inertial effects; do not interpret the aero increment as the complete dynamic load transfer.

9. A four-case interaction

Positive downforce coefficients are 0.10 baseline, 0.18 with A, 0.22 with B, and 0.31 with AB. Compute the interaction and its force at 100 km/h, A = 2.2 m².

Worked answer

IAB = 0.31 - 0.18 - 0.22 + 0.10 = +0.01. Dimensional interaction is 0.01 × 1039.74 ≈ 10.40 N. If variation in the paired cases is larger than that, the interaction is unresolved.

10. A yawed inlet

A car travels at 120 km/h in a 6 m/s perpendicular ambient wind. Find relative airspeed and yaw magnitude. What road velocity should a car-fixed moving-ground boundary represent?

Worked answer

Airspeed is √(33.333² + 6²) ≈ 33.87 m/s, yaw ≈ atan(6/33.333) = 10.20°. Road speed is 33.333 m/s directly rearward, with no lateral component. Wheel rolling speed follows road motion, not the airspeed magnitude.

11. A drag-power budget

Added drag area is 0.040 m². Find added still-air power at 200 km/h in density 1.225 kg/m³. Compare it with a project limit of 3 kW.

Worked answer

q ≈ 1890.43 Pa, ΔD ≈ 75.62 N, ΔP = 75.62 × 55.556 ≈ 4.20 kW. The candidate exceeds that stated project limit. Lower-speed performance or downforce benefit does not make the constraint disappear; revise the package or explicitly revise the requirement.

12. An honest uncertainty statement

Cd means are 0.350 and 0.346. Window bands are ±0.006 and ±0.005, and grid sensitivity has not been tested. Write your conclusion.

Worked answer

“The observed drag difference is -0.004, but it is not resolved by the available temporal evidence and numerical sensitivity is unquantified. Longer sampling and paired refinement/domain checks are needed before claiming a gain.” Do not combine the displayed bands as independent statistical standard deviations; they are window-spread diagnostics.

Appendix B / Field guide

Glossary and formula sheet

Aerodynamic balance
Distribution of equivalent aerodynamic vertical load between axles, based on force and pitching moment.
Angle of attack / incidence
Angle between a section reference chord and its incoming air; a mounting angle need not equal local incidence.
Aspect ratio
b²/S; span/chord for a rectangular wing. Declared using wing planform geometry.
Attachment
Boundary-layer state in which flow continues along a surface rather than forming an extended separated region.
Boundary layer
Near-wall region with strong velocity gradients influenced by viscosity and turbulent transport.
Blockage
Projected body area divided by tunnel cross-section; a confinement indicator, not an accuracy certificate.
Camber
Curvature of an airfoil's mean line.
Canard / dive plane
Small front-corner aero surface with direct load and possible downstream flow effects.
Cd / Cl
Drag and signed lift coefficients based on q and a declared reference area.
Centre of pressure
Effective force location for a chosen force/moment decomposition; unstable when the relevant force approaches zero.
Cp
(p - p∞)/q∞, a local static-pressure coefficient.
Diffuser
Expanding passage intended to recover static pressure; its flow depends on the complete inlet-to-exit system.
Downforce
Positive downward aerodynamic load, defined in this course as -L.
Drag area
CdA, in m²; useful across different area conventions.
Dynamic pressure
q = ½ρU², in pascals.
Endplate
Surface near a wing tip that changes spanwise flow and loading; it also adds drag and yaw sensitivity.
Flow pass
In the browser solver, a car-length/freestream-speed scale used for simulation effort; local stepping is pseudo-time.
GCI
Grid convergence index, a discretisation-uncertainty reporting method under stated refinement assumptions.
Ground effect
Aerodynamic changes due to proximity and interaction with the ground.
Gurney flap
Small trailing-edge tab used to modify load and pressure recovery.
Induced drag
Drag associated with a finite lifting system's induced flow; not all drag on a wing.
Laminar / turbulent
Different flow regimes of transport and fluctuations; neither word alone establishes attachment.
LES / RANS / URANS
Different turbulence modeling/resolution strategies; their predictions require their own qualification.
Mach number
U/a, the speed ratio relative to sound.
Moment origin
Reference position used for torque integration. Changing it changes the reported moment by a force-arm term.
Pareto frontier
Nondominated feasible candidates in a multiple-objective comparison.
Pressure recovery
Increase in static pressure as flow decelerates; a demanding recovery can provoke separation.
Rake
Longitudinal floor inclination associated with front/rear ride-height difference.
Reynolds number
UL/ν; compare using a declared characteristic length.
Separation
Detachment of a boundary layer; diagnose with surface-flow and off-wall evidence.
Splitter
Front horizontal surface that can produce downforce from a pressure difference.
Streamline / pathline / streakline
Instantaneous field tangent / one particle trajectory / particles released from a source. They coincide only in steady flow.
Total pressure
For the limited incompressible relation, static plus dynamic pressure; losses distinguish wakes from reversible acceleration.
Wall function
Model relating near-wall mean flow and stress without resolving every inner-layer scale.
y+
uτy/ν, a solution-dependent nondimensional wall distance.
Yaw
Relative-flow angle in plan view; ambient wind and road motion must be separated.
